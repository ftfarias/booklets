## Resposta 1 (Claude-Opus-5)

### [Raciocínio]

I'll research this piece for you. Let me dig into the specific copy and the binding itself.

### First, the book itself

What you have a photograph of is the upper cover of a binding on one of the great Art Deco livres d'artiste. Here is what the reference literature says about the edition:

Paul Fort, Les Ballades françaises. Montagne, Forêt, Plaine, Mer. Lyon, Cercle lyonnais du Livre, 1927. In-4 (approx. 256 × 197 mm).

- François-Louis Schmied (1873–1941) did everything except write the text: he conceived the ordonnance (layout), the typography and the illustration, and printed it on his own Stanhope press in his Paris atelier, with Pierre Bouchet as graveur-pressier (this was reportedly the last book Bouchet worked on as Schmied's employee before setting up on his own at Boulogne-sur-Seine).
- Illustration: colour wood engravings — 4 large title bandeaux with column decoration, 24 full-page compositions, 28 bandeaux and 4 bouts de ligne (Christie's counts "54 compositions, of which 26 full-page" — the counts vary depending on how the double-page spreads are tallied).
- Typography: entirely in capitals, in two sizes only, the text block enclosed in a tight gilt fillet, doubled at head or tail — a deliberate evocation of medieval manuscripts, and one of Schmied's most radical typographic exercises.
- Print run: 120 nominative copies for members of the Cercle lyonnais du Livre + 45 "collaborator" copies numbered 1–45 (= 165 total). The paper is famously heavy, which is fine for loose sheets in a portfolio but stiff once bound — a known headache for binders of this title.
- References: Carteret, Illustrés IV, p. 165 ("belle édition très cotée"); Mahé II, col. 81; Ward Ritchie, Art Deco: The Books of François-Louis Schmied, p. 29; Gordon N. Ray, The Art Deco Book in France, pp. 101–102; Nasti B15.

Your copy — one of the 45 exemplaires de collaborateurs, with an autograph poem by Paul Fort and five original gouaches by Schmied — is at the very top of the edition. Those gouaches are almost certainly the maquettes Schmied painted before cutting the blocks: colour models used to plan the successive wood-block printings. A copy like that is exactly the kind of book a collector commissions an exceptional binding for, which explains what you are looking at.

### The binding: style

The decoration is squarely in the Art Deco "reliure de création" tradition founded by Pierre Legrain and continued by Schmied himself, Rose Adler, Paul Bonet, Georges Cretté, Creuzevault, Jean Dunand and Jean Goulden. Its hallmarks are all here:

- Geometry as structure: concentric circles crossed by long diagonals, a composition that reads as a graphic poster rather than as a traditional frame-and-centrepiece.
- A single naturalistic motif drawn from the book, flattened and stylised — here a dragonfly on a white disc surrounded by ripples/haloes (the pond, the moon, the "plaine"/water imagery of Fort's ballads). Taking the cover design from the illustrations inside is the doctrine Ray describes for Schmied's own bindings.
- Contrast of materials rather than ornament: matt black grained goatskin against burnished gold, silver-white and copper metallics, plus a crackled white field. This "material collage" aesthetic is pure 1925–1935 Paris (and it is also the register in which the best late-20th-century binders work when they re-bind Schmied).

### The binding: techniques you can identify in the photograph

- Couvrure: full black grained goatskin (maroquin/chagrin), the pebble grain deliberately left prominent as a matt foil to the metals.
- Leather mosaic (mosaïque): the wings, body and red wing-spots are pieces of coloured/metallic leather. Two possible techniques, and you should look at the original in raking light to tell them apart:
  - mosaïque incrustée (inlay) — the covering leather is cut away and the piece let in flush;
  - mosaïque appliquée (onlay) — the piece pared down to 0.1–0.2 mm and laid on top.
  - The greenish-gold wings look like metallic kid or metallised goatskin; the reds like thin morocco onlays.
- Listels / bandes of metallic leather: the long straight diagonals in silver, gold and copper-bronze at lower left and lower right appear to be strips of metallised leather inlaid (not tooled), which is why they have that soft metallic sheen and slight surface texture, unlike the crisp gilt lines.
- Dorure à l'or fin (hot tooling with genuine gold leaf) for:
  - the concentric circles, made with a graduated set of gouges (curved tools) or a compass-mounted fillet — laying eight or nine perfect concentric rings by hand is the real technical feat of this cover;
  - the straight fillets (filet on a wheel);
  - the fine wing venation, done with tiny hand tools/azurés or a very fine pointed fillet directly on the leather onlays.
  - The whitish "silver" lines are probably palladium or platinum leaf, which 20th-century binders substituted for silver because silver tarnishes.
- The crackled white disc: the most likely candidate is coquille d'œuf — crushed eggshell mosaic set in lacquer, the technique Jean Dunand popularised in Paris and used on bindings and panels for Schmied. Alternatives to check: a craquelé lacquer panel, an inlay of parchment/vellum deliberately crazed, or galuchat (stingray shagreen). Under a loupe, eggshell shows discrete polygonal plates with dark mortar-like joints — which is what the photo suggests.

### Related bindings on this same title (useful for comparison)

- Schmied's own binding (he bound only his own books, signing "F.L.S." on the turn-in): full red morocco, gilt tooling on black and maroon onlays, gold-and-black moiré silk endsheets — described in Ray, pp. 101–102.
- A period unsigned binding in beige morocco with a great "rainbow" of coloured dots and a champlevé enamel plaque by Jean Goulden set into the centre (wave motif, for Une ballade de la mer), from the Baron Gourgaud collection; reproduced in Victoria Arwas, Art Deco (1980) and Bernard Goulden, Jean Goulden (1989), p. 107 — sold at Drouot.
- Paul Gruel binding on a copy at the Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (acc. 92.1033, acquired by Henry Walters before 1931).
- A geometric mosaic binding by Honegger, dated 1998 (Christie's, Paris).

### What I could not confirm

I could not identify the binder of your copy from the image — dragonfly/eggshell decorations of this type were made both in the 1920s–30s and by contemporary relieurs d'art working in Deco idiom, so I'd rather not guess. To pin it down:

- Look for a gilt signature, normally on the lower edge of the front turn-in (chasse) or at the foot of the front doublure, often with a date; sometimes also blind-stamped.
- Photograph the doublure and endleaves, the spine, the edges (tranches dorées sur témoins?) and the chemise/étui or emboîtage — dealers describe these systematically, so they're the fastest route to a match in auction archives (Christie's, Sotheby's, Ader, Artcurial, Gazette Drouot, Bibliorare, Edition-Originale).
- If it is a modern binding, the design might be documented in the binder's own catalogue or in the Reliure Contemporaine / ARA-France exhibition catalogues.

## Pergunta 2

I don't have this work, I just got a picture of it, and I'm interested to understand the techniques used to draw, carve, inlay the elements, with such details and quality

## Resposta 2 (Claude-Opus-5)

### [Raciocínio]

Good — that's a more interesting question anyway, because the techniques on this cover are documented craft procedures you can actually learn, independent of who made this particular one.

Below is the sequence a binder would follow to produce something like this, with the specific difficulties at each stage.

### 1. The design comes first, but is executed last

A binding like this is decorated after the book is sewn, boarded, backed, covered and its turn-ins pared and pressed. But the design has to be decided before covering, because it dictates choices upstream: whether the boards need to be recessed for a plaque, whether the leather must be a smooth or grained skin, whether a doublure will be added to hide the back of inlays.

The design stage itself:

- A maquette at 1:1, usually in gouache on paper or card, sometimes several variants. This is where the geometry gets resolved — and note that this composition is ruler-and-compass geometry: a set of concentric circles struck from one centre, plus straight lines at fixed angles. That's not accidental. It matches the tools available.
- The critical constraint, and the thing beginners underestimate: you do not draw freely, you draw with the tools you own. A finisher's kit is a set of gouges (short brass tools each carrying an arc, sold in graduated sets, each tool being a segment of a circle of a given radius) plus straight filets/fillets on wheels, dots, and small ornaments. A design gets adjusted so that its curves correspond to arcs the workshop actually possesses. Which is why Art Deco covers are full of circles and straight diagonals — the tooling kit's natural vocabulary, used deliberately as a modern idiom.
- The design is transferred to a thin paper template (~40 gsm), which is positioned on the cover and tooled through, so the layout is marked on the leather without drawing on it. Centre lines, the compass centre and angular positions are marked out with dividers.

### 2. The mosaic: onlay vs inlay

The dragonfly and the metallic bands are mosaïque de cuir — leather mosaic (also called leather marquetry). Two distinct techniques, and the difference is invisible in a photo but decisive in practice:

- Onlay (mosaïque appliquée): the piece is pared extremely thin and pasted on top of the covering leather. Sally Lou Smith's classic account in The New Bookbinder argues for onlays precisely because they leave the functional covering intact.
- Inlay (mosaïque incrustée): the covering leather is cut through and lifted away, and the coloured piece dropped into the void so it sits flush. Structurally riskier, visually flatter, and traditionally the more prestigious.

The craft in both cases is paring. The onlay must go down to something like 0.1–0.2 mm — tissue-thin, translucent — otherwise you see a step at the edge and the whole thing looks stuck-on. That's done with a French or English paring knife on a litho stone, a spokeshave for larger areas, or a razor-blade machine (Scharf-Fix, Brockman) which excels at very thin work. Practical points that produce the quality you're seeing:

- Cut away blemishes before thinning, and "board" the skin (fold flesh-to-flesh and roll in four directions) to soften it so the knife doesn't veer.
- Work from the edges inward with short strokes; long strokes on partly thinned leather stretch it, bunch it and tear it.
- The shape is drawn in reverse on paper pasted to the flesh side, cut with a scalpel, then the paper is peeled off after pasting down.
- Onlay pieces are cut on a bevel, hair side down against a straight edge — that's how those long narrow metallic strips get edges that read as a crisp line rather than a ragged fibre.
- For strips, one trick is to paste the pared leather onto Perspex, let it dry (which stiffens and flattens it), then cut the strips and lift them off.
- Adhesive is wheat starch paste, thin, with blotter and light nipping to avoid a swollen edge.

The greenish-gold wings and the silver/copper bands are almost certainly metallised or metal-leaf kid/goatskin — commercially prepared skins with a metal surface — rather than paint. That's why they read as material rather than as ink.

### 3. The gold tooling

The concentric rings, the fillets, and the wing venation are dorure à l'or fin, hot tooling with genuine gold leaf. The process, in the order it happens:

- Optional paste wash (dilute starch) over the leather, to control absorption and raise the grain.
- Blinding in: a warm tool struck through the paper template on dry leather, then repeated without the paper to sharpen it, sometimes again with a cool tool on dampened leather and finally a hot tool on damp leather. The impression must be crisp but shallow — deep enough to define, shallow enough that light reflects out of it instead of being swallowed by the walls.
- Glaire — egg albumen (or a shellac-based proprietary size such as BS Glaire / Fixor) — painted into the bottom of the impression with a fine sable brush, dried, and a second coat applied.
- Gold leaf flipped onto a gold cushion, cut with the gold knife, picked up either onto the greased leather or onto the greased face of the tool, and the heated tool struck again exactly into the existing impression. Gold sticks only where there is both glaire and heat. Excess is rubbed away with a gold rubber or cheesecloth.
- Repeat where there are breaks; three layers of leaf for solid coverage on difficult work.

Two things about this cover are genuinely hard:

- The concentric circles. Each ring is built from many separate arc impressions with gouges, and every join has to be invisible. On a circle, error accumulates: a fractional misjudgement of angle or of where the previous arc ended is visible as a kink or a double line, and there is no undo — a mis-struck impression is permanent in the leather. Getting eight or nine truly concentric, evenly spaced rings is the sort of thing that separates a competent finisher from a master. (Some workshops use a fillet mounted on a compass arm for large circles, which trades one difficulty for another.)
- Tooling on top of onlays. The venation on the wings sits on leather 0.15 mm thick over another leather. Heat that would be right on 1 mm goatskin will scorch or cut through that. So the finisher is working with reduced heat, reduced pressure, and less margin, while needing more precision because the lines are fine.

Also note: the whitish "silver" lines are unlikely to be silver leaf, which tarnishes almost immediately. Palladium or platinum leaf is the standard substitute for a cool white metal.

### 4. The white crackled disc

This is the one element that isn't a bookbinding technique at all — it comes from lacquer work. The most likely candidate is coquille d'œuf: crushed eggshell set into lacquer, an old Vietnamese/Japanese technique that Jean Dunand learned via Seizo Sugawara in 1912 and made a signature of Art Deco, precisely because true white cannot be obtained in urushi lacquer. The method:

- Fragments of eggshell are placed one by one, with tweezers, into a bed of fresh lacquer — Dunand's workshop had a dedicated poseuse for exactly this, and he kept a chicken coop in the courtyard.
- Lacquer is then built up in very thin layers, each sanded, each taking days to cure (a piece could take 6–9 months and up to 40 layers, total thickness only 3–4 mm), then polished.
- The dark "mortar" lines between the polygonal plates are the lacquer showing between fragments — which is exactly what the texture in your photo looks like. Convex/flat placement and the density of fragments control whether it reads as cracked ice, marble or bone.

Because cured lacquer is rigid, on a binding it is normally made as a separate plaque let into a recess cut in the board before covering, not applied to a flexible cover. Dunand made lacquer bindings in exactly this way, and he collaborated with Schmied. Alternatives you should rule out under a loupe if you ever see the object: craquelé lacquer (no discrete plates), parchment/vellum deliberately crazed, galuchat (stingray shagreen — regular pearly tubercles, not polygons), or a champlevé enamel plaque (as Jean Goulden supplied for at least one binding on this very title).

### 5. Why this level of finish is possible at all: division of labour

Worth knowing as a student: French reliure d'art of this period was not a one-person job. The designer, the relieur (forwarder), the doreur (finisher), the mosaic specialist and the lacquer/enamel workshop were often five different people or firms. Pierre Legrain famously designed covers he could not execute and had them made by Canape, Stroobants, Cretté and others; Schmied worked with Dunand. So the "impossible" quality partly reflects an entire specialised trade behind one cover, each person doing only the thing they do all day.

### 6. Diagnosing technique from an object (or a good photo)

- Raking light across the surface: an onlay throws a tiny shadow at its edge; a true inlay does not.
- Gloss and depth: tooled gold sits in a depression and has a burnished, slightly mirrored bottom; metallic leather sits proud and has a soft, grainy sheen. Compare the fine circles against the wide diagonal bands in your image — they behave differently.
- Grain continuity: if the covering leather's grain runs through under a metallic element, it's an onlay of transparent-ish material or a tooled line; if the grain stops dead, it's an inlay or a thicker onlay.
- Under magnification: eggshell shows irregular polygons with dark joins; craquelé shows a continuous film split by drying stress.

### 7. Where to learn the actual procedures

- Sally Lou Smith, "Onlay Techniques", The New Bookbinder — the best short text on onlays specifically; the whole journal (Designer Bookbinders) is the key modern literature.
- Guild of Bookworkers Standards of Excellence handouts — freely downloadable; e.g. Feinstein's titling/gold-tooling handout gives the glaire-and-leaf sequence step by step with troubleshooting.
- Bernard Middleton, A History of English Craft Bookbinding Technique — for the historical technique of tooling.
- Edith Diehl, Bookbinding: Its Background and Technique — old but the clearest full workflow in English.
- Jeff Peachey's blog (Peachey Conservation) — the reference on paring knives, machines and technique, which is 80% of mosaic work.
- reliures.bnf.fr — the BnF binding database with a rigorous French glossary (mosaïque de cuirs, fers, filets, roulettes, doublure, chasse…), essential if you want to read French descriptions accurately.
- For the lacquer side: Félix Marcilhac, Jean Dunand: Vie et œuvre, and Alastair Duncan & Georges de Bartha, Art Nouveau and Art Deco Bookbinding.

If you want a realistic progression as a student: practise straight fillets in gold on scrap goatskin until they're unbroken; then a single circle with gouges; then concentric circles; then a two-colour onlay with a tooled outline. The onlay paring and the invisible arc joins are the two skills that gate everything on a cover like this one.
