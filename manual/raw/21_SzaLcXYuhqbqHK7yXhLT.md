# Conversa 21 — Fórmulas de encerramento para cólofon: alternativas a "Laus Deo"

## Pergunta 1

I notice many cólofon finish with "Laus Deo". I wants to put something like that, but more religious neutral. I thought Carpe Diem but it's overused. Do you have suggestions?

## Resposta 1 (Claude-Opus-5)

### [Raciocínio]

Colophons have a rich tradition of secular closing formulas, so you have plenty to choose from. A few groups, depending on the tone you want:

### Keeping the "Laus ___" shape

If you like the rhythm of Laus Deo but not the theology:

- Laus lectori — "Praise to the reader"
- Laus libris — "Praise to books"
- Gratias tibi, lector — "Thanks to you, reader"
- Ad maiorem artis gloriam — "To the greater glory of art" (a wink at the Jesuit AMDG)

### Classic scribal endings (fully secular)

- Explicit — the true medieval counterpart to incipit; "here it ends." Very authentic on its own.
- Finis coronat opus — "The end crowns the work."
- Feci quod potui, faciant meliora potentes — "I did what I could; let those who can do better." Elegant and humble; a real colophon favourite.
- Absolvi / Scripsi — "I have finished" / "I have written."

### About books and writing

- Habent sua fata libelli — "Books have their own destinies." Probably the most beautiful option for a colophon.
- Verba volant, scripta manent — "Spoken words fly away, written ones remain."
- Littera scripta manet — "The written letter endures."
- Nulla dies sine linea — "No day without a line."

### Farewell to the reader

- Vale, lector — "Farewell, reader."
- Valete — "Be well" (plural).
- Perlege et vale — "Read it through, and farewell."

### Playful / medieval scribal humour (these are genuine, attested colophons)

- Explicit, expliceat, ludere scriptor eat — "It's finished, let it be finished, let the writer go play."
- Explicit hoc totum, pro pena da mihi potum — "All is finished; for my pains, give me a drink."
- Nunc est bibendum — "Now we must drink" (Horace).
- Acta est fabula, plaudite — "The play is over, applaud."
- Cetera desunt — "The rest is missing" (nice if the book ends open-endedly).

### Non-Latin, if you want something quieter

- τέλος (telos) — Greek, "end."
- 完 or 終 — East Asian book/film endings.
- इति (iti) — Sanskrit, "thus it ends."

One warning: a few Latin tags that look neutral are actually scriptural, so avoid them if neutrality matters — Consummatum est, Quod scripsi scripsi, Fiat lux, Tolle lege, Deo gratias, Nunc dimittis.

If I had to pick one: Habent sua fata libelli for something resonant, or Explicit, expliceat, ludere scriptor eat if your book has a sense of humour.

## Pergunta 2

Phases from the bible are welcome, (Vulgata is the Latin version, right?). But I have Muslim friends and Protestants who says "Jesus is God", and of course atheists who loves my Plato dialogue editions. So it's fine something from a religious text, but I don't want to get it linked to a particular religion. Habent sua fata libelli is fantastic!

## Resposta 2 (Claude-Opus-5)

### [Raciocínio]

Yes — the Vulgate is St. Jerome's Latin translation (late 4th c.), and it's the version medieval scribes actually quoted, so it's the right register for a colophon. (The Nova Vulgata is a modern revision; wording and verse numbers differ slightly here and there.)

Your instinct is a good one: the trick is to quote from wisdom literature rather than from anything doctrinal. The sapiential books read as universal reflection, not as creed, and they're shared ground between Jews and Christians and broadly respected in Islam — while atheists reading your Plato tend to file them under "literature" rather than "religion."

### The obvious winner for a colophon

Faciendi plures libros nullus est finis
"Of making many books there is no end." — Ecclesiastes 12:12

This is almost too perfect. It's from the Preacher, it's wry rather than pious, and it sits beautifully next to Habent sua fata libelli.

### Other Ecclesiastes / Proverbs options

- Nihil sub sole novum — "Nothing new under the sun" (Eccl. 1:9). So thoroughly proverbial that most readers don't register it as scripture at all.
- Omnia tempus habent — "To everything there is a season" (Eccl. 3:1).
- Tempus tacendi et tempus loquendi — "A time to be silent and a time to speak" (Eccl. 3:7). Lovely as a closing line.
- Melior est sapientia fortitudine — "Wisdom is better than strength" (Eccl. 9:16).
- Cor sapientis quaerit doctrinam — "The heart of the wise seeks knowledge" (Prov. 15:14).
- Vanitas vanitatum, omnia vanitas — famous, but it may read as gloomy or as a memento mori motif.

### What to avoid if you want neutrality

Anything with Dominus/Deus in it, obviously, but also anything spoken by Jesus or written by Paul — Ubi thesaurus tuus, Scientia inflat, Fides, spes, caritas — those will read as specifically Christian. And Initium sapientiae timor Domini is a wonderful line but has the divine name in it.

### If you'd like to widen the frame

- تم الكتاب — tamma al-kitāb, "the book is complete." The standard closing formula in Arabic manuscripts. Not religious in itself, but a graceful nod to the Islamic book tradition, and it pairs handsomely with a Latin tag.
- इति — iti, "thus it ends," the classic Sanskrit terminal marker.
- τέλος — plain Greek "end," and appropriate to your Plato volumes.

A suggestion: you could use Habent sua fata libelli as your house colophon and vary the second line per volume. The full hexameter from Terentianus Maurus is worth knowing — Pro captu lectoris habent sua fata libelli, "according to the reader's capacity, books have their destinies" — which makes the reader, not providence, the arbiter. Rather a fitting creed for a publisher of dialogues.

## Resposta 3 (Claude-Opus-5)

### [Raciocínio]

Shakespeare is almost unfairly well-suited to this, because his epilogues are colophons — a speaker steps forward, admits the work is over, and asks the audience's indulgence. That's exactly the gesture a scribe makes at the end of a manuscript. So the epilogues are the first place to look.

### From the epilogues (the closest thing to a Shakespearean colophon)

- "Our revels now are ended." — The Tempest 4.1. Short, perfect, unbeatable.
- "As you from crimes would pardon'd be, / Let your indulgence set me free." — The Tempest, epilogue. The direct English cousin of feci quod potui.
- "If we shadows have offended, / Think but this, and all is mended." — A Midsummer Night's Dream, epilogue.
- "Thus far, with rough and all-unable pen, / Our bending author hath pursued the story." — Henry V, epilogue. Literally about the pen that finished the book.
- "In little room confining mighty men." — Henry V, epilogue. A wonderful line for a printed volume: great matter, small space.
- "Here our play has ending." — Pericles, epilogue.

### Endings and completions

- "The end crowns all." — Troilus and Cressida 4.5. Effectively Shakespeare's own finis coronat opus, which would rhyme nicely with your Latin line.
- "All's well that ends well; still the fine's the crown." — All's Well 4.4 (fine = end). Same idea, more music.
- "The rest is silence." — Hamlet 5.2. Superb as a bare explicit, though it does carry the shadow of a deathbed.
- "And thus the whirligig of time brings in his revenges." — Twelfth Night 5.1.

### On books, writing, and survival

- "Not marble, nor the gilded monuments / Of princes, shall outlive this powerful rhyme." — Sonnet 55.
- "So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, / So long lives this." — Sonnet 18. Nearly a printer's motto.
- "That in black ink my love may still shine bright." — Sonnet 65.
- "Which eyes not yet created shall o'er-read." — Sonnet 81. Lovely and slightly eerie.
- "Me, poor man, my library / Was dukedom large enough." — The Tempest 1.2.
- "Come, and take choice of all my library, / And so beguile thy sorrow." — Titus Andronicus 4.1.
- "Words, words, words." — Hamlet 2.2. If your sense of humour runs that way, it's a marvellous deadpan colophon.

### Two for the Plato volumes specifically

- "There are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio, / Than are dreamt of in your philosophy." — Hamlet 1.5.
- "There is no darkness but ignorance." — Twelfth Night 4.2.

### One outside Shakespeare but from his book

The First Folio's address "To the great Variety of Readers" gives you this, which is arguably the finest colophon line in English:

"Reade him, therefore; and againe, and againe." — Heminges and Condell, 1623

Keeping the original spelling makes it look every bit as antique as the Latin, and it addresses the reader directly, just as pro captu lectoris does.

On pairing: since Habent sua fata libelli is your constant, the variable line does best when it either answers it or contrasts with it. The end crowns all answers it in the same key; Our revels now are ended provides a softer, more human close after the Latin's cool detachment. And you could match the register to the volume — sonnets over poetry, Hamlet over philosophy, the Folio line over anything you want read twice.
